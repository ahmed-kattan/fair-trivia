Snakes and ladders game vs Ludo: two dice games with two kinds of luck

Ahmed Moaz · 25 October 2026

Both are dice-and-board games from the same part of the world, both are loved by families, and both can be played in a browser. But if you sit down at a Ludo board after an evening of the snakes and ladders game, you quickly notice that the luck feels different. Here is how I would explain the difference, with some numbers from the versions I built.

What do they have in common?

Each is a race. A die decides how far you move. A table of two to four can play either one. Both can be learned in a minute, and both let children beat adults. In both, a late reversal can turn a game around.

Where does the luck differ?

In snakes and ladders the luck is the whole game. You throw, you move the counter, and the board does the rest. There are no decisions. In Ludo, the die gives you a number, but then you choose which of your four pieces to move, and whether to capture, hide on a safe square or keep running. The luck decides your options, but you decide what to do with them.

This is the main thing to know when you pick one for an evening. If you want a game that asks nothing of you, choose the snakes and ladders game. If you want to feel clever, choose Ludo.

How long does each one take?

One counter on the snakes and ladders board needs 39.2 throws on average to finish. In Ludo each piece needs 20.8 throws on average from its entry square to home, but you have four pieces, and you first need a six to get each one out. Counting all four, a Ludo player makes dozens more throws than a snakes and ladders player, so a Ludo game for four tends to run from half an hour to an hour.

How do they treat the end?

Both use the exact finish. In snakes and ladders you must land on 100 with the precise number. In Ludo you must reach home with an exact throw too. The cost is similar, a few throws of waiting. But Ludo adds a second pressure: when you wait, an opponent can overtake you or capture a piece, so the end is tense for a different reason.

Can you hurt another player?

In snakes and ladders, never. Counters pass through each other and nobody can block or capture. In Ludo you can send a rival's piece back to base. That makes the game warmer and sharper. Some families love the rivalry. Others prefer the harmless quality of snakes and ladders, especially for younger children.

Which suits which table?

  • A family with a four-year-old: snakes and ladders.
  • A group of friends who like banter: Ludo.
  • A big table of up to eight: snakes and ladders, since Ludo seats four.
  • A video call where people can only look at their own screens: either, but snakes and ladders is easier to follow.

Where to try both

Both games are on Board It, a free site I make, so a family can try snakes and ladders online first and the sharper game next without leaving the page. Both run as multiplayer tables in a browser, for two players up to four in Ludo and up to eight in snakes and ladders, with no download.

A pairing for the evening

Start with snakes and ladders for the youngest players, and move to Ludo when they go to bed. The two games meet different moods, and a single evening can use both.

The limit of the comparison

The numbers describe single pieces. Real Ludo games include captures and blocking, which change the length. So treat the comparison as a guide to the feel of each game and not a precise timetable.
