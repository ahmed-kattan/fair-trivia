Games like Jackbox for a remote team: a 30-minute call plan with no shared screen

Ahmed Moaz · 29 October 2026

A manager asking about games like Jackbox for a remote team usually has a calendar problem as much as a fun problem. The slot is thirty minutes. People are on laptops, a few have cameras off, and one person cannot get software installed. This article gives a plan that fits those limits, using free online games with no download and a call you already have.

Jackbox is owned by its publisher and has nothing to do with my site. By the publisher's own description, players join from a phone or web device while everyone watches a shared host screen. On a team call, the call itself becomes that shared place.

What should a remote team game avoid?

Three things sink a team game quickly. The first is a long setup, since the first five minutes decide the mood. The second is a game that makes one person perform alone while others watch. The third is a game that needs everyone to be good at it. A quiz, a drawing round and a clue game all avoid these, because everybody acts together and a lucky guess counts.

What does the 30-minute plan look like?

My suggested run sheet, for a team of up to eight, is below. It suits a virtual game night over lunch as well.

  • Opening, five minutes: send the link and let everyone enter a name.
  • Middle, fourteen minutes: a Trivia Tavern quiz, which is listed at about that length.
  • Finish, ten minutes: a short Draw and Guess round.
  • Close, one minute: thank everyone and name the next date.

The drawing game needs trimming to fit. Rounds can be set to two, three, five or eight, and a drawing can run forty to a hundred and twenty seconds. Six players, two rounds and sixty seconds is twelve drawings of up to a minute each, so at most twelve minutes of drawing. That fits the slot, even with word choice and results.

Which game suits a team that does not know each other well?

The quiz. Nine categories and a Mixed option mean you can avoid topics that exclude people. Questions climb in difficulty, and the round ends on a numbers question that the nearest guess wins, so a person who knows nothing about the topic can still land close. Choose a topic with broad appeal, such as Food and drink or Geography, rather than a category that only one or two people know.

What about a team that is more comfortable?

Who's the Spy suits teams that have worked together for a while. Online, three to eight people can take part, each clue turn lasts twenty-five seconds and a clue tops out at twenty characters, so the pace stays quick. It rewards listening, which is a decent habit to practise in a meeting anyway.

One caution: none of those three games has a computer-played seat, so everyone you invite has to turn up. If one person is late, start with the people present.

How do you handle people with cameras off?

Each person has the game on a personal screen, so a camera is not part of it. Drawing and quiz games work with only voices and chat. You can tell people in the invite that cameras are optional, and the game will still work. I run these on Boardit, where nobody has to create an account, and a typed name is the whole sign-up.

Who should get the link?

Send the link only inside the meeting invite or chat. Anyone who has the code or link can sit at the table, so a team channel with outside guests is the wrong place for it.

What should you do afterwards?

Ask one question in the chat: which game would you like next? You will learn more about how your team wants to spend time from three answers than from a survey. Pick the same slot the following week, keep the same format and change only the game. A routine of thirty minutes is easier to keep than a one-off event.

Before the call starts

Open the game yourself five minutes before the call. Make the table, check the settings and have the link ready. The only thing a facilitator must not do is fumble the start.
