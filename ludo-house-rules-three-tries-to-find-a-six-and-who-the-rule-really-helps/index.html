Ludo house rules: three tries to find a six, and who the rule really helps

Ahmed Moaz · 5 November 2026

Of all the ludo house rules, this is the one families fight over. Without it, a player with nothing out throws once a turn and waits for a six. With it, they get up to three throws to find one. Some tables swear by it and others call it cheating. I added it as a switch in my Boardit lobby, and what follows is what it does to a ludo game and which player gains.

What exactly does the rule change?

A player who has no piece out on the track throws the die. A six brings a piece out and play goes on as usual. Any other number means another throw, until three have been made in that turn. If all three miss, the turn passes.

Nothing else moves. It starts switched off. The host turns it on in the lobby before play, and it stays fixed once the game begins. The Wikipedia article on the German relative of ludo, Mensch ärgere Dich nicht, describes the same idea as a commonly played variation and not as a base rule.

How much shorter is the wait?

Much shorter, and the numbers are easy. In my calculation a six turns up after six throws on average, with a median of four. Across runs of three throws, 57.9% hold no six at all, so a player who throws once a turn faces a slow opening and plenty of empty stretches.

Three tries put those three throws inside one turn. The chance of finding a six within a turn becomes one minus 57.9%, which is 42.1%. At one throw a turn it is one in six, or 16.7%. A failed turn also burns less table time, because three throws pass in the space of one.

When does the rule actually apply?

This is the detail most people miss. The rule is live whenever none of your pieces is out on the track. Pieces still in base count as not out. So do pieces that have already gone home.

The second case matters late in a game. Say three of your pieces are home and the last one sits in base. You are waiting on a six with nothing else to move, and the rule gives you three throws a turn instead of one. It softens the most frustrating moment in ludo, which is a finished army held up by a single piece.

Does it help the unlucky or the lucky?

It helps the unlucky. A player with a bad opening falls far behind under the plain rule, because everyone else is already moving while they wait. The extra throws narrow the gap between a lucky opener and an unlucky one.

It leaves the other wait alone. A piece one to six steps short of home still needs one exact number, and that wait averages six throws whatever the distance. So the rule shortens the start of the game and does nothing for the finish.

Does it speed up the game?

Almost certainly, though I have not measured by how much. The opening gets shorter, and so does the wait for a last piece. Each failed try also restarts the 30-second clock, so nobody is hurried through the three throws.

I would expect one side effect. Pieces come out sooner, so the track fills earlier and the first captures should arrive earlier. Whether that suits you depends on your table.

When would I switch it on?

  • With newcomers, who drift away during a long dull opening.
  • In Quick Ludo, where the wait for a six is a larger share of a short game.
  • On a call where the talk is the point and a run of empty throws is dead air.
  • With a mixed group, when one person has had a poor start and the table wants a close finish.

When would I leave it off?

When the table enjoys the old tension of waiting. For some groups the wait is part of the character of the game, and the rule would take it away. That is a fair reason to keep it off.

How do you try it?

Open a table at Boardit's Ludo, switch the rule on in the lobby and play one game each way. For ludo with friends I would play twice and decide by feel. Let everyone vote before the first throw, because the setting cannot change afterwards. Alongside Quick Ludo, bonus throws and captures, it makes a set of ludo rules you can mix to suit any evening of ludo online, with four players or two.
