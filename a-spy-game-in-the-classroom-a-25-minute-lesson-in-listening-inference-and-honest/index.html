A spy game in the classroom: a 25-minute lesson in listening, inference and honest bluffing

Ahmed Moaz · 19 October 2026

A spy game online such as Who's the Spy is not what most teachers think of as a classroom activity. But it trains skills that a curriculum asks for: careful listening, drawing inferences from partial evidence, giving a precise short description and explaining a decision. In twenty-five minutes, a class can play three rounds and talk about what they learned. Here is a plan for older primary and secondary students.

What does the game train?

  • Listening: the spy and the detectives must hear every clue.
  • Precision: a clue has to be short, true and not too revealing.
  • Inference: from several clues, a student must work out who does not know.
  • Argument: accusing someone requires a reason.
  • Honesty about bluffing: students discuss why a game allows a lie and real life does not.

How do you organise the class?

Tables of four to eight students, one device per student or one shared phone per table. Choose a category that suits the lesson, such as animals for a science class or places for geography. Appoint a scribe for each table, who notes the clues for the debrief.

What is the timetable?

  1. Minutes 0 to 5: explain the rules and the aims.
  2. Minutes 5 to 20: play three rounds. A table of six uses up to five minutes of clues before the first chance to call a vote, and about two more for the vote and the reveal; many rounds are shorter.
  3. Minutes 20 to 25: debrief.

What questions drive the debrief?

  • Which clue made you suspicious, and why?
  • Which clue was brave, giving a lot without giving it away?
  • How did the spy decide what to say?
  • Was anyone unfairly accused? How did that feel?
  • What would make a clue more precise?

How do you adapt it?

For younger students, use simple categories and one spy per table, and let the teacher join as a civilian. For older students, use two spies, and ask them to write a paragraph after the game explaining how they deduced who was the spy.

For a language lesson, play in the target language, so that every clue is a vocabulary exercise.

What does a good round look like in class?

The room goes quiet for the clues, then erupts at the vote. Students who rarely speak find themselves explaining a hunch to the table. The student who was the spy describes the pressure of saying something plausible with no information, and the others hear what it is like to bluff. That conversation is the real lesson: the difference between a game lie that everyone agreed to and a lie that hurts.

Teachers often find that students who struggle with written work do well at inference games, because the task rewards attention and social sense. It is worth noticing who shines.

How do you keep it kind?

Set one rule: accusations are about clues, not people. "That clue was vague" is allowed. "You always lie" is not. The game is built so that nobody is eliminated, which helps: every student plays every round, and the vote ends it.

Which tools do you need?

A link, a few devices and a timer if you want to keep the lesson tight. On Boardit, a free imposter game online with friends, guests need no account, so a class can be playing in a couple of minutes.

What are the limits?

The words are dealt by the game, so a teacher cannot add lesson vocabulary. The activity trains listening and reasoning, not subject content. If you want content, pair it with a short written task about the topic of the category.

A closing thought

Students rarely forget a lesson in which they were suspected. The debrief turns the fun into learning.
