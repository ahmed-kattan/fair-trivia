Virtual trivia on a video call: split the screen, mute for the question, unmute for the reveal

Ahmed Moaz · 6 November 2026

The easiest way to ruin virtual trivia on a video call is to let people shout. One loud answer and the next ten seconds are wasted. The second easiest is to share a screen when nobody needs one. Here is the setup I would use for online trivia with friends on a call, with the quiz running beside it, and why.

Do you need to share a screen at all?

No, and you should not. In Trivia Tavern every player opens the game in a browser on their own device and answers there. The trivia questions arrive on each screen, and a player's choice stays hidden until the question closes. So there is nothing to share. If one person put the game on the call, the rest would be watching a copy of something they could already see, and it would eat bandwidth and make the video worse.

Use the call for faces and voices. Use the game for the questions and the answers. A trivia quiz needs nothing else.

How do you split the screen?

The cleanest layout uses two devices. Join the call on a laptop and open the game on a phone, or the other way round. Then your eyes move between two screens and nothing overlaps.

If you have only one device, put the two windows side by side. Give the call a narrow strip, enough to see a few faces, and give the game the wider part. On a phone, the same idea holds if your phone can show two apps at once. If it cannot, use the call for audio only during the match. You lose the faces for 14 minutes and keep the voices, and that is a fair trade.

Test this before the quiz, not at the start of it. Ask everyone to try the layout in the five minutes before.

When should people be muted?

During the question, because the voice that blurts out the answer is the only leak in the system. The game keeps every answer private. A spoken one is the only way to give it away, whether on purpose or from excitement.

So here is the three-step rhythm I suggest:

  1. Mics off while the question is open. The clock is 10, 15, 20 or 30 seconds, depending on the lobby.
  2. Mics on at the reveal. Each answer comes with a short explanation, and that is the moment for the groan or the cheer.
  3. Mics off again when the next question opens.

A match has the pauses built in. After each question the game shows the answer for several seconds and then the scores, so there are windows of roughly ten seconds when talking is safe and fun. That is a comfortable rhythm, and it keeps the call alive.

What about delay?

Video calls carry a small delay, and it differs for every person. Someone who laughs at a reveal may seem to laugh before you have read it. That is not a bug in the game. Ask people to wait a beat. In a race, the order of right answers is set by when the server receives each answer, and not by what shows on a call. If your group finds the delay annoying, the gentler pace removes most of the pressure.

How do you handle the closest-number question?

Each round ends on a question where players type a number and the nearest guess scores most. This one is perfect for a call, because it invites banter. After the reveal, let everyone say their guess out loud and see how far off they were. It is the one moment where I would encourage talking before the answer is final, but only after the clock stops.

Who runs the call?

One person hosts the game and presses start. Another can run the call and keep the mics in order. If you are alone in that role, do the setup once, state the mute rule at the start, and trust people.

Boardit's Trivia Tavern works as a trivia night for up to eight devices at once, so a call of up to eight will fit. If more people are on the call, pair them up on shared devices.

A line to say at the start

"Answers on your own screen, mics off while the clock runs, mics on for the reveal." Once everyone has heard those words, the quiz runs itself. Remind them after the first round, then stop reminding.
