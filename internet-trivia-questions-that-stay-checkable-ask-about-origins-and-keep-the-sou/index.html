Internet trivia questions that stay checkable: ask about origins, and keep the source

Ahmed Moaz · 10 October 2026

Internet trivia questions go stale in two ways. The answer can change: a platform renames itself, a record falls. Or the answer stays put while the page that proved it disappears. Most advice on writing quiz questions deals with the first problem. This piece is about the second, because on the internet the evidence rots faster than the facts.

I run a browser trivia quiz game and wrote its topic pages, and internet and memes is one of its topics. The game's question bank was rebuilt and switched on in the last week of September 2026, so its internet questions are recent. Their sources are what will age first.

How many web pages disappear?

More than most quiz writers assume. In May 2024 the Pew Research Center published a study of pages sampled from an archive of the web, 2013 to 2023. Of the pages that existed in 2013, 38% could no longer be reached a decade later. A quarter of all the pages collected over those years were gone as of October 2023.

Reference lists fared no better. Pew found that 54% of Wikipedia pages had at least one link in their references that pointed to a page that no longer exists. News pages and government pages had broken links too, at 23% and 21%.

What is link rot? It is that pattern seen from a single question: a link that worked when you wrote it and leads nowhere by quiz night. A question whose only source has vanished can no longer be checked, and an unchecked question is how a quiz with friends ends in an argument.

Internet trivia questions should ask where it started

The answer that cannot change is the origin. Who made something, what it was first called, what it grew out of: those facts are fixed the day they happen. Meme origins work the same way. A meme's popularity moves every week; the forum or video it came from does not.

My own internet and memes page says its questions cover "origins more than punchlines". I counted to see whether that holds. Of the 39 sample questions on the public page, 16 ask who made something, what it was first called, or what inspired or started it. That is 41%, by my own reading of each question.

For internet history trivia, that is the habit worth copying. Write the question so the answer is an event in the past, and it stays true however the platform changes.

Prefer the source written at the time

An origin question needs a record behind it. The strongest record is one written by the person involved, close to the event.

Take one well-documented case. What is Godwin's law? Mike Godwin's own wording is that as an online discussion grows longer, the chance of a comparison to Nazis or Hitler approaches one. He explained it in a 1994 essay for Wired, and in it he says he set out in 1990 to spread it as a counter-meme. That gives you a date from the author himself:

In what year does Mike Godwin say he began spreading his law about online arguments? A) 1985 B) 1990 C) 1995 D) 2000

The answer is B. The question also names its own source, which helps when someone at the table disagrees.

How to fact-check a trivia question before quiz night

  1. Open every source link the week of the quiz, not the month you wrote it.
  2. If a link is dead, look the page up in the Wayback Machine, run by the Internet Archive, and note the archived address beside the question.
  3. If you cannot find the original or an archived copy, rewrite the question around a fact you can still check, or drop it.

Those three steps cover much of how to write trivia questions about the internet that survive a challenge, whether the quiz is trivia online or in a pub. Meme trivia is the hardest case, because so many memes began on pages that have since gone. A meme with no surviving origin page makes a weak question, however famous it is.

What a source check can't promise

My 16-of-39 count is one reading of one public page, with my own definition of an origin question. Pew's figures describe pages from 2013 to 2023. Neither says how long any particular link will last, and a source that opens today can fail tomorrow. Check again close to the night.
