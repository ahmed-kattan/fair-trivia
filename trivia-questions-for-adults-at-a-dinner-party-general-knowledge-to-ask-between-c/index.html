Trivia questions for adults at a dinner party: general knowledge to ask between courses

Ahmed Moaz · 2 November 2026

A dinner party needs a good game, and the right game is one that never takes over the evening. Trivia questions for adults fit that role if you ask one between courses instead of running a round. This is a set of ten, written to be asked one by one, with no pictures, no paper and no phones until the last question. It is general knowledge, the kind everyone has heard something about.

The ten questions

  1. Which car brand has a three-pointed star as its badge?
  2. Which mural on a Milan refectory wall shows Jesus at a meal with his twelve apostles?
  3. Who wrote Pride and Prejudice?
  4. Which code of dots and dashes was used on the telegraph?
  5. How many years of marriage does a golden wedding anniversary mark?
  6. Which of the seven wonders of the ancient world is the only one still standing?
  7. Which composer wrote his Ninth Symphony when he was almost completely deaf?
  8. Who painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel?
  9. Which number is thought unlucky in China and Japan because it sounds like the word for death?
  10. Closest year: in which year was the first human heart transplant performed?

The answers

  1. Mercedes-Benz. The three points stand for engines on land, at sea and in the air.
  2. The Last Supper, by Leonardo. It was painted on a dry wall, so it began to flake within decades.
  3. Jane Austen, who published her novels without her name.
  4. Morse code. Samuel Morse was a portrait painter before he worked on the telegraph.
  5. 50 years. Silver is 25 and diamond is 60.
  6. The Great Pyramid of Giza. There is no firm evidence that the Hanging Gardens existed at all.
  7. Ludwig van Beethoven.
  8. Michelangelo, between 1508 and 1512.
  9. Four. In China, eight is lucky, and the Beijing Olympics opened at 8:08 pm on 8 August 2008.
  10. 1967. Christiaan Barnard led the operation in Cape Town, in December.

How do you pace trivia between courses?

Ask one question after each course is cleared, and give the answer straight away. Do not wait for hands up or a score. The aim is a pleasant interruption, not a contest. Ten questions fit a starter, a main, a dessert and the coffee with room to spare, so ask two or three at a time in the longer gaps.

Keep a running tally in your head, or on a card that sits near the host. Count a point for each right answer, and let anyone call out. Whoever has the most when the coffee comes wins a small prize, such as choosing the next dinner's theme.

What makes a good dinner-party question?

It has a short answer. It does not need a screen. It leads to a sentence of conversation. Question 6 is the clearest example: the answer is the Great Pyramid, but the real fun is the second half, where the table talks about which wonders were lost and which may never have existed.

Avoid anything that sorts the guests by education. A question about a composer is fine, because someone at the table knows it, and a hint from the host can pull in the rest. A question about a university or an exam is not.

Which questions will your guests argue about?

Number 9 may start a conversation. People have their own views on lucky and unlucky numbers, and some will know other examples. Let it run for a minute. Then read the note and move on.

Number 5 is a good one for anyone who has been to a wedding. A guest who knows silver and diamond can reason to the one between them. A guess of 40 misses by a little, and 50 is a small win.

What if the party is large, or half the guests are far away?

Ten people around a table is the sweet spot for asking out loud. With more, or with a few guests on a video call, a shared quiz is easier. Boardit's Trivia Tavern takes up to eight players, each on their own phone or laptop, and it has General knowledge as one of its nine categories.

It is a trivia quiz you can start with friends who have just finished eating, and a good fit for after dessert, when people want to stay at the table but not keep talking about the same things. The game scores the round, and the host can eat.

How do you adapt the set for your own guests?

Swap questions to suit the table. If you are hosting people who love music, replace question 5 with one about a composer or a band. If the guests are mostly from one country, add a question about a place they will all know. The shape matters more than the contents: two gentle questions, five in the middle, two harder and a number to finish.

Keep a spare or two on a card, in case a question lands badly. A guest who has just been told they got it wrong about their own field will not enjoy the next course. A spare lets you move on without comment.

Dinner-party questions also differ from a pub trivia night in one way: the host is a participant. You may know the answers because you wrote the sheet, so let another guest hold it and read each question. It is easy to hand over, and everyone then gets a turn at the answers.

Keeping score without ruining dinner

Do not turn the evening into a tournament. If the guests want a real contest, move to the sofa after dessert and play a full round of online trivia. Dinner itself is the wrong time for a close finish.

For the final question, the number, ask everyone to say a year at the same moment. The nearest wins, and the table has a last small laugh. Then pour the coffee.
