Ludo online or the board at home: what changes, and what does not

Ahmed Moaz · 23 October 2026

Some families swear by the old wooden ludo board, with its rattling die and scarred counters. Others play ludo online with cousins on three continents. Neither is wrong, and the games are very close. But a few things do change when a computer holds the board, and knowing them helps you choose. Here is a fair comparison, built on how my own table behaves.

What stays exactly the same?

The shape of the game. A six to leave base, bonus throws, captures, safe squares, pairs and the exact finish are all the same. The luck is the same too: one die, one in six for a six, 20.8 throws on average for a piece to get home. If you could swap a family from a real board to a screen without telling them, they would play the same game.

What does the computer do for you?

Several chores disappear:

  • It rolls the die, and everybody sees the result at once.
  • It counts squares, so nobody miscounts a move.
  • It enforces the rules, including the third six and the exact finish.
  • If a throw leaves only one legal move, it plays that move.
  • If a throw leaves no legal move, it ends the turn.
  • It runs a turn clock, so a distracted player does not freeze the game.

These take away the commonest arguments of a family game. A ludo game that settles its own disputes leaves the players free to enjoy it.

What does it take away?

The physical parts. There is no rattle, no counter to hold, no moment when a child reaches over and moves a piece. Some players feel the tension of a throw more when they hold the die. Others miss the way a board sits in the middle of a table and draws the family around it.

It also removes the small human agreements. At a real board, a parent might let a child retake a throw. A table does not bend, which is good for fairness but sometimes less kind.

Which suits which occasion?

  • Family in one room: the board at home.
  • Family in several places: ludo online.
  • A school or club with many tables: online, for speed of setup.
  • A quiet evening with grandparents: whichever they find easier.

How does speed compare?

Online play is usually faster per turn, since there is no counting and no rule checking. A game listed at thirty to sixty minutes can run closer to the lower end on a screen, and a real board with chatty players closer to the upper end.

What about cost and effort?

A real board costs money once and sits on a shelf. A free ludo game online costs nothing, but needs a device and a connection, and for a family on a tight budget that is often the deciding point. Setup takes a minute on a screen and five at a table, including the hunt for the die.

Can you do both?

Yes, and many families do. Use the online table for the long-distance games and the board when you are together. The rules are the same, so a child who learns online can play on the real board at once.

Where can you try the online version?

The free table at Boardit, which I build, seats two to four and runs in a browser. You can play ludo with friends who live far away, and the ludo rules are applied by the table. Try one game online and one on the board, and ask the family which they prefer.

A tip for a smooth first game

Do a quick test turn with everyone before you start, so that nobody is confused by the die button. A minute of testing saves ten of confusion.
