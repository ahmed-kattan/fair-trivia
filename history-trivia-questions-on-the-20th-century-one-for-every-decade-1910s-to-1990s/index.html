History trivia questions on the 20th century: one for every decade, 1910s to 1990s

Ahmed Moaz · 29 October 2026

A good way to build history trivia questions on the twentieth century is to take one event from each decade. The table then has a feel for the order of things, not just a pile of dates. Below are nine questions, one for each decade from the 1910s to the 1990s, and a tenth that settles ties by asking for a year.

Nine questions, one per decade

  1. 1910s: Which ocean liner sank after hitting an iceberg in 1912?
  2. 1920s: Which leader founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923?
  3. 1930s: Which British prime minister signed the Munich Agreement with Hitler in 1938?
  4. 1940s: Which 1945 conference saw Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin plan the shape of post-war Europe?
  5. 1950s: Which revolutionary leader took power in Cuba in 1959?
  6. 1960s: Which US president was shot in Dallas in 1963?
  7. 1970s: Which Moon mission suffered an explosion in 1970 but brought its crew home alive?
  8. 1980s: Which space shuttle broke apart soon after launch in 1986?
  9. 1990s: Which Soviet leader won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1990?

The tie-break

  1. Which year saw the Wall Street Crash that began the Great Depression? Whoever is closest takes it.

The answers

  1. The Titanic. Its wreck was not found until 1985, almost four kilometres below the surface.
  2. Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, whose title means "Father of the Turks".
  3. Neville Chamberlain, who flew home promising "peace for our time". War came within a year.
  4. The Yalta Conference. Yalta is in Crimea, on the Black Sea.
  5. Fidel Castro, who led Cuba for almost five decades, until 2008.
  6. John F. Kennedy, the youngest person ever elected US president, at 43.
  7. Apollo 13. Its crew were brought home safely.
  8. The Challenger. The teacher Christa McAuliffe was among the seven crew killed.
  9. Mikhail Gorbachev, whose reforms helped bring the Cold War to an end.
  10. 1929. The worst day, 29 October, is called Black Tuesday.

Why ask one question per decade?

Because people remember a century in lumps, and a trivia quiz is easier to follow when the questions move forward in time. Ask a table "what happened in 1938?" and many will freeze. Ask which British prime minister signed a deal with Hitler, and the same people know the answer. A decade label is just enough of a hint to place the event without giving it away.

It also keeps the set fair across ages. Someone who was alive in 1986 may know the Challenger at once, and someone born in 2008 may know Apollo 13 from a film. Neither wins every time, which is what you want at a mixed table.

How hard is it?

Numbers 1, 6 and 7 are easy. Numbers 2, 8 and 9 are medium. Numbers 3, 4 and 5 are the hard ones, because they name a person or a meeting that school lessons often skip. The tie-break is easy to guess close to: anyone who knows the crash came before the Second World War is already within a decade.

I would read the tie-break only if two or more people finish level. If no one is level, skip it. A good tie-break should settle something, not pad the evening.

Does it work as a trivia night round?

Yes, and it sits nicely behind a round on inventions or the ancient world. If your group is spread out, you can also play trivia online with friends: Boardit's Trivia Tavern plays much the same way as the set above. Each round climbs from easy to hard and finishes on a number question, much like the tie-break, and the final round counts double.

The History category is one of nine, and up to eight players answer each question at once, on their own phones. Because everyone answers each one, nobody sits out waiting for a turn.

Dates and sources

The events and dates above are drawn from the Boardit question bank, where every entry has a reference page; I went back to those pages on 27 October 2026. I stuck to headline events because a quiz answer needs one clear fact. This is not a full history of the century, and a stronger set would add more places than I have here.
