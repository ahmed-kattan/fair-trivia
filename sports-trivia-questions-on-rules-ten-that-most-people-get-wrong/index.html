Sports trivia questions on rules: ten that most people get wrong

Ahmed Moaz · 31 October 2026

Most sports trivia questions ask who won. This set asks what the rule says, and rules are where confident people trip. Ten questions come first, drawn from football, tennis, basketball, cricket, baseball and rugby. After the answers I explain why each slip is so common, and how to host a rules argument without it going wrong.

The ten questions

  1. Can a goal be scored directly from a throw-in in football?
  2. Is every football pitch the same size?
  3. If a defender tries to clear the ball and sends it into their own net, whose goal is it?
  4. A football player is ahead of every defender when a team-mate takes a throw-in to him. Is he offside?
  5. How much of the ball must cross the goal line for a goal to count?
  6. In the NBA, how many steps may a player take after stopping a dribble?
  7. In tennis, is a ball that lands on the line in or out?
  8. In cricket, a ball hit over the boundary after bouncing first scores how many runs?
  9. In baseball, a batter with two strikes hits a foul ball. Is that a third strike?
  10. In rugby union, may a player pass the ball forward?

The answers

  1. No. A goal cannot be scored directly from a throw-in.
  2. No. Under the rules a pitch can be anywhere from 90 to 120 metres long.
  3. It is a goal for the other team, even if the defender meant to clear it.
  4. No. There is no offside offence when a player receives the ball directly from a goal kick, a corner kick or a throw-in.
  5. All of it. The whole of the ball must pass over the goal line, between the posts and under the crossbar.
  6. Two steps, once the dribble has stopped.
  7. In. A ball is out only if none of it touches the area inside the lines or the line itself.
  8. Four. A ball that clears the boundary without bouncing is worth six.
  9. No. A foul ball counts as a strike unless the batter already has two.
  10. No. The ball may be passed sideways or backwards, although a player may kick it forward.

Why do these rules trip people up?

I think most of the slips come from how we watch. We see a ball cross a line on a replay and decide it was a goal, when the rule needs the whole ball. We see a player ahead of the defenders and shout offside, forgetting the three restarts that cancel it. Rules that need two facts at once are the ones people half-remember.

Question 7 is a different kind of slip. A common belief is that the ball must land inside the court. The rule says that touching the line is enough, so a line call is an "in" call.

Question 9 makes a foul ball behave differently depending on the count. A rule that changes with the score is hard to guess if you have not watched much baseball.

A word on the tennis serve

A related tennis rule is the let. If a serve clips the net and still lands in the correct box, it is void and the server takes it again. The rule texts call it a let, or a net service, and it is a nice follow-up question for a table that finds the first ten too easy.

Where should you look a rule up?

Start with the governing body of the sport, whose rulebook is the final word, and then use a reference page for a plain-language summary. For football, the Laws of the Game are published by the body that maintains them, and the wording of the goal-line rule above comes straight from there. Leagues and age groups sometimes change details, so name the version you used when you write the answer down.

What makes a good rules question?

I look for three things. The rule must be written down somewhere you can link. The common belief must be different from the rule, so there is something to learn. And the answer must be short enough to say in one breath. A question that needs a paragraph of explanation belongs in a rules discussion, not in a quiz.

How should you settle an argument at the table?

Decide before the first question which source settles disputes. If someone says their league plays differently, accept it for their league and give the point for the rule as written in the source. A rule dispute is the one place a quiz can turn sour, so a calm, written-down policy helps. It works the same in a pub quiz and on a phone.

Can you play it online with friends?

Yes. Boardit's Trivia Tavern lets 2 to 8 people share a trivia quiz online by link, and the Sports category is one of 9. Online trivia has one benefit here: each person sees the same four answers at once, so a rules argument turns into a points table, not a shouting match. If you want to practise alone first, there is a solo mode.

Try it on the next match you watch

At the next game, write down any call that makes someone at home groan. After the game, look the rule up in the source. You will often find that the call was right and the groan was wrong, which is a good reason to ask rules questions at all.
