Hosting a property game for a stream: making a table viewer-friendly

Ahmed Moaz · 15 October 2026

A property trading game suits a stream because every turn holds a visible decision: buy, bid, trade or build. It also has traps. A table that haggles can stall on air, an invite shown on screen can let strangers in, and the watch feature has limits worth knowing before you promise anything to an audience. Below is a run of show for streaming Property Rush, an independent Monopoly alternative for up to six players, built only from how the game works. I would seat three or four, and everything here applies when you play online with friends.

Two kinds of watching

Your video audience sees only what you share. The game has its own watching feature as well, and the two are separate.

The host can switch on spectators from the table. It is off by default. Once on, up to 20 people can open the watch view with a watch invite. They cannot play and they cannot see hidden cards. A watcher's screen asks the game for fresh state every 4 seconds, so a watcher can trail the players by a few seconds. If you are reading out a roll while a watcher is still looking at the last one, that is why.

Keep the invite off screen

The table invite lets whoever holds it take a seat, so it should never sit visible on a stream. Make the table private before you go live, so that it stays out of the open-tables list.

Treat the watch invite the same way: the first 20 signed-in people to open it fill the watching places. I have not checked what your lobby screen shows at the moment you go live, so open a test table and see what is visible before you do. Do this on the device you will actually stream from, not on a second one.

Pace the table

Property Rush has a turn clock: each turn has a minute, then your own three-minute bank for the whole game. A countdown shows in the last 20 seconds of the minute, and a move does not reset it. When both run out, a bot finishes the turn safely and buys nothing. So answer chat between turns, not during yours, and keep a hand on the roll.

The starting purse is one of five options, $500, $1,500, $2,000, $2,500 or $5,000, and $1,500 is the default. Less cash at the start squeezes the table sooner and shortens the game. The game card says about 45 minutes, and nobody counts rounds, so a game ends when a single player remains. That means a stream slot needs its own cut-off, and nothing in the game decides who won at your cut-off.

A run of show that I would use for a two-hour slot:

  • 0:00 to 0:10: introduce the table and explain the three rules viewers need (buy a city, a full country doubles the rent, a declined city goes to auction);
  • 0:10 to 1:10: play, with a smaller purse so the game is likely to finish;
  • 1:10 to 1:30: buffer for a long game, then call it unfinished and say if you will play on next time;
  • 1:30 to 2:00: chat and next week's plan.

Twenty minutes before you go live

  • Choose the starting purse. Write down the cut-off time.
  • Check the host switches: auctions, double rent on full countries, even building. Each one changes what you will have to explain.
  • Open the table, make it private, and keep the lobby off the capture.
  • Switch spectators on and join from a second device.
  • Agree with your co-players that nobody pastes the invite into chat.
  • Decide who narrates trades, since watchers cannot see them, and who watches the chat while you play.

Say the decision aloud

Auctions are the strongest moments for an audience, because the whole table bids in the open. For every purchase, a good narration names the price, the rent it will charge, and the country it moves toward. A country held in full doubles the base rent on its unbuilt cities, so that last piece is the one worth explaining.

What the watchers will not see

The watch view shows the public board: who owns what, the purses, the dice. Jail cards are a private hand, so watchers see none. Trades are also left out, so watchers see no offers on the table. Narrate your trades aloud, because a watcher cannot read them, and say who offered what to whom. Your own cash is yours to show or hide on your own stream.

One more thing is worth explaining on air. A bill a player cannot pay is a debt, not a knockout. The player goes below zero and has to mortgage, sell or trade to climb back, and is out only by declaring bankruptcy or by going quiet. A viewer who misses that will think someone has lost when they have not.
