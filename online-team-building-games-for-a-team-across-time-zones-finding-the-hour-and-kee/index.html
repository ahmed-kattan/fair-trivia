Online team building games for a team across time zones: finding the hour and keeping the game fair

Ahmed Moaz · 31 October 2026

A team spread over three continents has a problem that a team in one office never meets. The game is live, so somebody is always playing at a bad hour. Online team building games can still work for a team like that, but only if you plan for the person who joins late, drops out, or plays at breakfast while a colleague plays at midnight. This article is about those plans: how to pick the hour, which games forgive a missed minute, and what to do when nobody can make it.

How do you pick an hour that is fair?

Start with the overlap, not the ideal. A video call has to suit the people on it, and a game night is no different. Write down each person's working day in a shared sheet and find the stretch where most people are awake and on duty. If that stretch is thin, use two slots and alternate them, so the late hour does not always fall on one person.

Then protect the length. A game at the edge of someone's day should be short. I would put the long games in the bigger overlap and keep the early or late slot for a quick one. Fifteen minutes of free online games at 8 p.m. is a favour. Fifty minutes at 8 p.m. is a cost, and people stop turning up.

Which games forgive a missed minute?

Three of my games keep moving when a player steps away. In Property Rush a turn is 60 seconds, then the player spends from a three-minute reserve that has to cover the entire game. Once the reserve is gone, a computer-controlled player finishes the turn. After two missed turns, that stand-in keeps the seat warm until the person returns, and no one is sent away for being absent. Ludo and Snakes and Ladders have 30-second turns and use the same stand-in for a player who misses two.

In the drawing game a latecomer can join a game that has already started, so ten minutes late does not mean locked out. Trivia Tavern and Who's the Spy have no stand-ins, so every seat has to be a person who is present. For a team with patchy attendance, I would lean on those three.

What if nobody can make a live hour?

Some teams have no real overlap at all. A live game is not the answer for them, and I would not pretend otherwise. There are two small things that come close. The quiz game has a solo mode, and Boardit Daily offers five questions a day, so each person can play when it suits them and post a result in the team chat. That is a shared habit, not a shared game, and it is weaker than being together. It can still keep a thread of play running between live sessions.

How should the host handle the start?

Send the link early and keep the room open for the whole slot. On Boardit, which I make alone, a typed name is the whole entry, so someone arriving from a phone at the last minute is in at once. There is no download to wait for.

Say plainly when the call opens: if you are late, join anyway. If your internet drops, come back and rejoin. Those two sentences remove most of the anxiety around live play.

How can a rota stop the late shift feeling punished?

Do not keep a permanent leaderboard. The person who always plays at midday would win every season, and the person on the late shift would stop caring. Treat each session as a fresh game, and rotate who chooses it. Where a prize exists, a joke trophy that moves from week to week is enough.

Rotate the slot too. If the Asia team joins early on Monday, let the Europe team join early on the next call. A team notices fairness quickly.

What is the simplest plan to start with?

Run one 25-minute live call a fortnight in the widest overlap you can find. Open with Snakes and Ladders, which is pure luck and takes 10 to 25 minutes. Keep the room open for stragglers, and let the daily questions fill the gaps. Then ask, once, if the hour works for everybody. If the answer is no, change the hour before you change the game.
