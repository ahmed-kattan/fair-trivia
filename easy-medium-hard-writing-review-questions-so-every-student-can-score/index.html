Easy, medium, hard: writing review questions so every student can score

Ahmed Moaz · 17 October 2026

Define each level by the share of the class you expect to get it right, counting lucky guesses. Deal a round mostly easy and medium, hard ones last. Test every question before it goes in. Then think about points, because weighting by difficulty quietly decides who gets to score.

I keep a question bank for an online quiz game, Boardit's Trivia Tavern; this is the method I use there, adapted for a review lesson.

Start from the guess floor

With four options, a student who knows nothing still gets 25% right on average. So a question only a quarter of the class answers correctly isn't hard. It is broken: a wrong key, a trick, or a topic nobody was taught. My own writing brief makes the same call.

Above that floor, testing research gives a few useful marks. Halfway between a blind guess and certainty is 62.5%. The University of Washington's assessment office, citing Frederic Lord's 1952 paper in Psychometrika, puts the ideal for a four-option item a little higher, at about 74%. Barak Rosenshine, summing up classroom studies in American Educator (2012), puts the optimal success rate during practice at about 80%: students are learning, and still stretched.

So for review: easy means nearly everyone, medium sits around those figures, and hard stays clearly above the floor.

Make the fact hard, not the options

My brief puts it bluntly: difficulty comes from the fact, never from the options. A question should be hard because of what the student has to know, not because the options look alike; four years close together make a lottery. Here is one subject at three levels.

  • Easy: Which gas do plants take in from the air to make their food? Carbon dioxide (oxygen, nitrogen, water vapour).
  • Medium: In which part of a plant cell does photosynthesis take place? Chloroplast (mitochondrion, nucleus, cell wall).
  • Hard: During photosynthesis, which substance is split to release oxygen? Water (glucose, carbon dioxide, chlorophyll).

In each, the wrong options are the same kind of thing as the right one and roughly the same length. Carbon dioxide sits in the hard question on purpose: a student who half-remembers the equation will reach for it. Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez drew their 2002 list of 31 item-writing guidelines from 27 testing textbooks and 27 studies and reviews (Applied Measurement in Education). It says the same: keep options about equal in length, make every distractor plausible, build distractors from students' typical errors, and avoid "all of the above".

Run the cover-the-options test

Before a question goes in, cover the four options and read only the question. A student who knows the fact should be able to say the answer. A student who doesn't should not be able to work it out from the wording.

A common failure is a question that holds its own answer. "What is the process in which plants use light to make food?" gives away photosynthesis to anyone who knows that "photo" means light. Haladyna and colleagues list a close cousin, the clang association: a right option that repeats or resembles words in the question. My brief bans any word of the answer from the question for the same reason.

One more rule from the brief: when torn between two levels, pick the easier one. Too many hard questions send quiet students out of the game early.

Deal the round easy first

In the quiz game I build, the default Mixed setting deals every round 40% easy, 40% medium and 20% hard, with the easy ones first and the hard ones last. (The host can pick one level instead.) A ten-question round is four, four, then two.

Starting easy means every student has scored before the questions begin to separate the class. Ending hard means the last minutes still decide something.

Decide what a point is worth

My game pays 100 points for easy, 200 for medium and 300 for hard, so a hard question is worth exactly three easy ones. For one ten-question round dealt that way:

  • Four easy, four medium and two hard come to 1,800 points.
  • A student who gets only the four easy ones right has answered 40% of the questions correctly but holds 400 points, or 22% of the total.
  • A student guessing every answer blind can expect 25%, or 450 points.

So under difficulty weighting, the student who knows the basics and leaves the rest blank finishes below what a blind guesser can expect. If that same student guesses the other six, the expected score rises to 750.

That weighting suits a game, where the end should stay exciting. A review lesson has a different job: if every student should be able to score, give one point per question, or tell the class plainly that a guess costs nothing.

Let the answers relabel the questions

Your levels are guesses until students answer. Afterwards, count how many got each question right and move it to the level it played at. Check any that almost nobody got right before using it again.

Limits

The same three-band rule works for a quiz at game night and for party games online with friends.

My questions are written for mixed groups of friends and family at a party, not for a class following a syllabus. The bands above are rules of thumb from testing research, and I haven't measured how students answer any of them.
