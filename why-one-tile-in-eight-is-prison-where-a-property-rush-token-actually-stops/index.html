Why one tile in eight is Prison: where a Property Rush token actually stops

Ahmed Moaz · 7 November 2026

Most people think a token stops everywhere about equally. I ran the numbers and it does not. In a simulation of 3,000,000 turns of one token, Prison took 12.0% of all the places a token comes to rest. An even spread would give each of the 40 tiles 2.5%, so Prison gets nearly five times its share.

I ran this on 17 October 2026 against the rules in the game's code: two dice, doubles roll again, three doubles in a row go to Prison, the Go to Prison tile sends you there, and two decks of cards. I counted only the tiles where the token ends a turn.

Why Prison is so busy

Four things send you to Prison. The Go to Prison tile never counts as a stop, because the token is moved straight on. Three doubles in a row do it. Three cards do it, one in Treasure and two in Surprise. And once you are in, you can spend up to three turns there.

My model lets a token wait up to three turns, and I think that explains why the tile collects so many stops while the token waits.

Which cities are busiest

Prison aside, the busiest city in the simulation was Osaka at 3.26% of stops, then Paris at 2.80%, Venice at 2.79% and Toronto at 2.73%. All the cities together took 54.3% of stops. That tells you something practical: land you can buy is where the token spends most of its time, so owning a good set early pays.

Where I would use this

Boardit's Property Rush is an independent Monopoly alternative with 40 tiles, 22 cities in eight countries, two to six players and no round limit. The last player with money wins. When you are choosing between two cities of similar price, the one with the higher stop rate has more chances to collect rent.

Why it matters at the table

Two of the default rules make Prison matter. Leaving Prison costs you the turn, and an owner in Prison collects no rent. So a token that sits there is not only wasting time; if it belongs to a player with several cities, those cities are quiet too.

That is also a reason to keep a card that lets you out. Treasure and Surprise each hold a pardon, and in a long game one of them is worth more than a small city.

Prison is square 10, and Start is square 0

The board has forty tiles, numbered 0 to 39. Start is tile 0, Prison is tile 10, Vacation is tile 20 and Go to Prison is tile 30, so the four corners are ten tiles apart. That is why a token that has just been sent to Prison starts its next lap from tile 10, a quarter of the way round, and why Prison and the tiles after it get so many visits.

What the number is not

These are numbers for the default dice. The optional Speed Die adds a third die and changes the spread, and I have not simulated that. The numbers also describe one token alone, not a table where players buy tiles and change what a card does.

Still, the main finding is easy to remember. Prison is one stop in eight.
