Ludo online with a 30-second turn: a thinking routine that fits the clock

Ahmed Moaz · 2 November 2026

Thirty seconds sounds short until the die is in your hand. I set that clock on my Boardit table, and it is the same for every ludo game, because a clock that changes from table to table is one more thing to argue about. If you play ludo online with a timer, the useful question is not how to think faster. It is which turns need any thinking, and what to look at in the ones that do. This article answers that, and it says plainly what happens when the clock wins.

What happens when the clock reaches zero?

The table plays the turn for you. It never skips it. A player who has not yet thrown gets a throw made for them. A player who has thrown but not picked a piece gets a piece picked.

The pick follows a fixed order, the one a person glancing at the board might use. Take a rival if one is on offer. Failing that, bring a piece home. Failing that, bring one out of base. Failing that, move the piece furthest along.

I chose to play the turn for one reason. A skipped player never throws the six needed to leave base, so the game would quietly become unwinnable for them while three others waited out the clock. A played turn leaves them somewhere to pick up from. The price is that the clock will never decline a capture to protect a piece you were saving. It is a poor stand-in for you.

How many of your turns need thinking?

Fewer than you would expect. A turn only asks for a choice when two or more pieces can move. If just one can, the table moves it at once. If none can, the turn passes.

At the start nothing can move until a six arrives. In my calculation a six turns up after six throws on average, and the median is four. Near the end the same thing happens in reverse, because a piece waiting for an exact number moves only when that number appears. So the clock really bites mid-game, when several pieces are out and each throw has two or three possible uses.

A three-question routine for the busy part of the game

Before you move, ask these in order.

  1. Can I take a piece? That means stopping on a rival who stands alone on an ordinary square.
  2. Is a piece of mine one to six squares ahead of a rival? Any given throw hits it one time in six, which is 16.7%. Beyond six and up to twelve, the rival needs a six first, so the risk drops to about 2.8%.
  3. Can I reach a safe square or the home column? Eight of the 52 squares are safe, which is 15.4% of the track.

Three questions, three glances. Usually one of them settles the move. I would guess the routine takes ten seconds once it is a habit, though that is my estimate and not a measurement.

Does a bonus throw get its own 30 seconds?

Yes. The clock restarts on every throw, including the extra one you earn from a six, from a capture or from bringing a piece home. If the host has switched on the three-try rule for finding a six, every failed try restarts it as well. So the clock limits one decision. It is not a budget for a whole turn.

That helps if you worry about a long chain of sixes. Each throw gets a fresh half minute, and three sixes running cost you the turn anyway.

What if you really are away?

A turn that the clock has to play is counted against a running tally. Play your own next turn, however late inside its 30 seconds, and the tally goes back to zero. Let the clock act twice without a move from you in between, and a bot takes your seat until you return. Your pieces and your place stay yours, and any move of your own hands the seat back.

You can feel all of this in one practice game at Boardit's Ludo, which is free to open.

Habits that make 30 seconds feel long

  • Read the board while other people play. By the time the die reaches you, the danger is already counted.
  • Before you throw, note which of your pieces is exposed. Then the number only has to be matched to a job.
  • Keep a hand near the die, so a distraction costs seconds and not the whole turn.
  • Settle the ludo rules before the first throw. A rules argument during someone's turn burns more seconds than any decision.

These habits matter most in ludo with friends, where the chat is half the evening and the clock is easy to forget. They matter just as much with four players as with two players.

Thirty seconds, used well

I do not think of the clock as pressure. It is a promise to the other players that the board keeps moving. Spend your thinking where it pays, on the turns with a real choice, and let the easy turns pass quickly.
