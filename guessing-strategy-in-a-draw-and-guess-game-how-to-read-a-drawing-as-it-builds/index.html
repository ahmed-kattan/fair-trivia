Guessing strategy in a draw and guess game: how to read a drawing as it builds

Ahmed Moaz · 22 October 2026

Most people think of a draw and guess game as a test for the drawer. It is just as much a test for the guesser. The player who reads a half-finished drawing and types the right word in the first few seconds takes the biggest share of the points. Here is how to read a drawing as it builds, with the numbers that make speed worth the effort.

Why is speed worth so much?

A guess is worth 100 plus 400 times the fraction of time left. At 80 seconds that is five points per second, so a guess with 70 seconds left scores 450, with 40 left 300 and with 10 left 150. The first correct guesser keeps all of it. The second gets about 56%, the third 38%. A guess in the first ten seconds is worth more than three guesses at the end.

What should you do while the drawer starts?

Look at the first stroke, not the whole picture. Is it a circle, a line, a rectangle? Is it big or small? Where on the canvas is it? These three facts rule out many words immediately. A large circle on the left could be a sun, a ball or a face. A long horizontal line could be a road, a river or a horizon.

How do you use the dashes?

Count them. The number of dashes and the gaps tell you the length and the number of parts. A word with two parts, such as nine letters and four, rules out most single words. A word with eight letters in one part is probably a thing, not a phrase. Keep the pattern in your head as you watch.

When should you guess?

Early and often. A wrong guess costs nothing, and the chat is not a penalty box. If you have a plausible idea after three strokes, type it. If the guess is one letter off, you will be told privately that you are close, and that is useful information. Guess three words in the first ten seconds, and one may hit.

How do you narrow down?

  1. After the first stroke: think of categories (animal, building, tool).
  2. After the second: use the dashes to eliminate.
  3. After the third: type your leading candidate.
  4. After the fourth: type the next one.

By the fifth stroke, the drawing has usually given its secret to someone, so the early guessers have the advantage.

What does the chat tell you?

It shows what others are guessing, but not the right answer, which is hidden. If several wrong guesses are circling the same idea, the drawer is probably close and a variation may hit. If guesses are scattered, the drawing is unclear, and you can think about what it might represent that no one has tried.

What about hints?

Letters appear as the clock runs down. They are a safety net, not a plan. If you are waiting for the hint, you are scoring 150 when you could be scoring 400. Use hints to confirm a hunch, not to find one.

How do you get better?

Play a lot, and watch how drawers think. After a few games you learn that "a circle with two lines" is usually a clock, and that "a big rectangle with a line across" is often a book. A table of friends who draw the same way learns each other's code, and that speeds up everyone.

Where can you practise?

At Board It, a free site I make, you can play a drawing game online with friends, two to twelve players in a browser. It is a fast guessing game, and a good party game for a video call. Play three games and keep track of your first guess each time. The habit of guessing early is learned by doing.

A closing note

The player who wins is rarely the strongest drawer. It is the one who is willing to be wrong quickly.
