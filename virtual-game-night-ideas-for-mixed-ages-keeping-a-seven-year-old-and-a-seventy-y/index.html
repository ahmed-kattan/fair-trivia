Virtual game night ideas for mixed ages: keeping a seven-year-old and a seventy-year-old in the same game

Ahmed Moaz · 28 October 2026

A call with three generations makes a fine evening and a hard design problem. A child wants speed and silliness. A grandparent wants calm. A teenager wants to win. The virtual game night ideas that work for this crowd are not about finding one perfect game. They are about evening out the odds, so that nobody wins by default and nobody loses by default. Here are three ways to do it.

Can luck be the great leveller?

Yes, and the numbers show how well. In my simulation of the Classic board of Snakes and Ladders, the first seat wins 26.0% of four-player games. A perfectly even split would be 25%. So being first gives a tiny edge and nothing else: skill plays no part. A seven-year-old and a seventy-year-old start with identical chances.

The host can pick Classic, Ladder Garden (more ladders) or Snake Pit (more snakes). For a mixed crowd, Ladder Garden keeps the mood friendly. It takes 2 to 8 players, lasts 10 to 25 minutes in the listing, and gives each turn 30 seconds.

How do you make a skill game fair?

Use teams, and pair old with young.

With Draw and Guess, the drawing is the skill, and the guessing is a shared job. Put a child and a grandparent on one device, with the child drawing and the grandparent shouting guesses. Then adjust the settings:

  • Time: the options run from 40 to 120 seconds. Use 120 for a slow table.
  • Hints: up to three letters. Give the full three.
  • Word choices: from two to five. Fewer choices means less to read.

Everyone can then contribute, and the winner is the household that works together.

What about a quiz?

Quizzes favour the fast and the well-read, which is exactly what you do not want here. Trivia Tavern starts as a race by default, with the biggest reward for the fastest correct reply. For a mixed table, take the gentler pace, which is one tap away, so the points are not a sprint. Then pair players, so the child reads the question aloud and the grandparent supplies the answer.

Every round finishes with a closest-number question, and the nearest guess takes it. That one is a gift to a mixed table, since a guess can come from anyone, and a wild guess sometimes wins.

What if the ages are far apart and the group is large?

Split by role, not by age. Let the children be the artists and the adults the guessers in one game, then swap in the next. Seats are rarely the problem, since Draw and Guess takes up to twelve and can even admit a latecomer mid-game. The harder part is attention. Keep each game under half an hour and put a stretch break between them, because the youngest will run out of patience before anyone else.

Who chooses the game?

Rotate the choice by age. The youngest picks first, then the oldest, then everyone in turn. It gives each person a stake in the evening, and it stops one strong voice from steering. If two people want different games, play both, and keep each to twenty minutes.

What keeps the call itself friendly?

Mute microphones when not speaking. Ask the oldest guest to place the device on a stable surface. Let children walk away for a minute without comment. And keep sessions short: forty-five minutes is plenty with the youngest, and ninety with only adults.

A rule for the grown-ups

If you are the strongest player in the room, play the game as though you were the worst. Aim for a close finish. When a child wins, congratulate them loudly and let the next game be theirs to choose. The goal is that everyone leaves wanting to come back, and for that, a fair table matters more than a perfect score.
