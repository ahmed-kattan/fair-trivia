Icebreaker games online: five-minute warm-ups, and which of my six are too long

Ahmed Moaz · 1 November 2026

A warm-up has to be small, even on a video call. Five minutes, then the real meeting starts. Most lists of icebreaker games online forget that, and suggest games that take half an hour. I make six games, so I checked how long each one takes, and only two of them fit a five-minute window. Here is the honest sort.

Which of the six fit in five minutes?

Two do, with the right settings.

Quick Ludo is the first. The host chooses two pieces for each player instead of four, and a game runs about five minutes. It seats 2 to 4 players, so it suits a small group. Turns are 30 seconds, which keeps it moving.

Draw and Guess is the second. The host can pick two rounds and 40-second drawings. With four players that is 4 × 2 × 40 = 320 seconds, a little over five minutes at most. It seats up to twelve, but the maths grows with the table, so a bigger group needs a shorter plan or a split.

Which ones are too long?

The rest. Trivia Tavern is about 14 minutes, Who's the Spy about 20, and Snakes and Ladders 10 to 25. Property Rush is about 45, and full Ludo 30 to 60. Those are fine for a social slot of their own. They are not warm-ups, and a host who starts one "just to open the call" will lose the first half of the meeting to it.

Does the size of the group change the answer?

Yes, because drawing time grows with the number of players. With three players, two rounds of 40-second drawings come to 3 × 2 × 40 = 240 seconds, four minutes at most. With six it is 6 × 2 × 40 = 480 seconds, eight minutes, which is no longer a warm-up. So the drawing game is a warm-up for a small group, and a main event for a larger one.

Quick Ludo does not scale the same way. It stops at four seats, so for a team of ten it is a side activity for a few volunteers, not an opener for all. If you have a big call, use a typed check-in for everyone and let the game wait for a longer slot later in the week.

What do you do if you want something shorter still?

Skip the game. A one-word check-in takes two minutes and needs no setup: ask everyone to type the last thing they ate, watched or listened to, and read a few out. It is quick, and it is not a game, but it does the same job.

Some teams like a rule of three: three words about your week, typed into the chat all at once and read in a row. It works for a group of any size, and the quiet people do not need to speak.

How do you run a five-minute game without it taking ten?

Prepare the room before the call, the way you would for any game night. Open it, set the rounds and the time, and put the link in the call chat at the start so people can click as they arrive. Boardit, the site I make on my own, asks a guest for a typed name only, so there is nothing to stall on, no download and no password. All six are free online games.

Then use a stop time. Say "we stop at minute five", and stop. If the game is not finished, call the leader and move on. Nobody remembers a game that overran, but they remember a meeting that began late.

What should the host say?

Two sentences. "This is a five-minute game to wake us up. It does not count for anything." Then start. A long explanation takes longer than the game.

What is the one rule?

Pick the game before the call, not during. A host who asks the group "what shall we play?" has just spent three of the five minutes.
