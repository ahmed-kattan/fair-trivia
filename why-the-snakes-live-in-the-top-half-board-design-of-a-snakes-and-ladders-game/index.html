Why the snakes live in the top half: board design of a snakes and ladders game

Ahmed Moaz · 6 November 2026

Look at a snakes and ladders game board for a minute and you start to see a pattern. The ladders begin low and the snakes begin high. It is not an accident. A board designer is shaping a story, and the shape tells you how the game will feel from start to finish. I mapped every snake and ladder on the board I use.

Where do the snakes start?

The ten snakes have their heads on squares 16, 47, 49, 56, 62, 64, 87, 93, 95 and 98. Nine of the ten are above square 45. Only the snake at 16 lives in the lower half. Three of them, at 93, 95 and 98, sit in the last ten squares of the board.

So the board gets more dangerous as you approach the end. A counter that has crossed the middle has not escaped; it has entered the zone where most of the snakes live.

Where do the ladders start?

The nine ladders begin on squares 1, 4, 9, 21, 28, 36, 51, 71 and 80. Six of the nine have their foot below square 40. The early game is full of chances to climb, which gives every counter a quick start. After square 50, only three ladders remain, at 51, 71 and 80.

The effect is a game with a hopeful beginning and a nervous ending. You are lifted early, then tested late.

How big are the drops and climbs?

Add up the length of every snake and every ladder, and a small imbalance appears:

  • All ten snakes together drop a counter 242 squares if each were used once.
  • All nine ladders together lift it 210 squares.
  • The longest snake is 87 to 24, a fall of 63 squares.
  • The longest ladder is 28 to 84, a climb of 56 squares.

The snakes are a little bigger overall, which is why the average counter needs 39.2 throws instead of the 33.3 a bare board would take.

Why put the big hazards near the end?

Because that is where tension lives. A snake at 98 only matters to counters that are about to win. It turns the last stretch into a series of small cliffhangers. If all the snakes were in the early squares, the leader would pull away safely and the game would feel decided by square 50.

The board is a script. Early on, ladders offer hope. In the middle, the long ladder at 28 and the snake at 49 produce big swings. Near the end, three snakes close together hold the finish.

Can you draw your own?

Yes, and children enjoy it. A good exercise is to give a class a 10 by 10 grid and ask them to place seven snakes and six ladders. Before they play, ask them to predict where the game will slow down. Then let them test it. Most children put the biggest snake somewhere they think is dramatic, which usually means near the end, the same instinct the printed boards follow.

If you want to see this board in action, Boardit, my own site, has a free table where you can play snakes and ladders online with friends, from two players up to eight. Watch where the sudden changes happen and compare with the map above.

What a designer would change

A designer who wanted a shorter game could move the late snakes earlier, or add a second long ladder near 70. One who wanted a crueller game could add snakes between 90 and 99. Every change shifts the story, and none of them changes the fact that the die decides.

A parting thought

The printed rule is the same in every home: ladders up, snakes down. The board's design decides what kind of evening you have. That is a nice thing to point out to children before the first throw.
