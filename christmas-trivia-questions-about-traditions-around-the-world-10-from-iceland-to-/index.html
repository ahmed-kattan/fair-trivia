Christmas trivia questions about traditions around the world: 10 from Iceland to Japan

Ahmed Moaz · 28 October 2026

Christmas is the same date on most calendars and a different holiday almost everywhere you go. These ten Christmas trivia questions are about customs that make people say "wait, really?": a log that gives out sweets, thirteen pranksters and a straw goat that keeps getting burned. I wrote them myself and read a named page for each. Question 10 asks for a year, and the closest guess takes it.

Ten questions, mostly easy, a few not

  1. In Japan, which fast-food chain's meal became a national Christmas custom after a 1970s campaign?
  2. How many Yule Lads visit Icelandic children in the nights before Christmas?
  3. In Catalonia, children beat a log with sticks and sing to it. What do they hope it will leave?
  4. How many dishes belong on the Polish Christmas Eve supper, Wigilia?
  5. What Disney special is shown on Swedish television at about 3 p.m. on Christmas Eve?
  6. What animal does the Swedish city of Gävle build as a giant straw figure every year?
  7. In which month does the Christmas season gradually begin in the Philippines?
  8. How many dawn Masses make up the Filipino tradition of Simbang Gabi?
  9. On which date do some Eastern churches that use the Julian calendar celebrate Christmas?
  10. In which year is the first firmly dated image of a Christmas tree, from Turckheim in Alsace?

Answers, one custom at a time

  1. KFC. The article says a 1970s campaign made it a national custom, and notes that Christmas is not a national holiday in Japan.
  2. Thirteen. They arrive one by one over the 13 nights before Christmas, and children leave shoes on the windowsill.
  3. Sweets, nuts and small gifts. The log is the Tió de Nadal. Children feed it from 8 December and beat it on Christmas Eve or Day.
  4. Twelve. The meal is meatless, and an extra place is set for an unexpected guest.
  5. From All of Us to All of You, shown at 3:05 p.m. on Christmas Eve every year since 1960.
  6. A goat. The first one went up in 1966 and burned down on New Year's Eve that year. By December 2025, 43 of the 60 goats had been destroyed or damaged in some way.
  7. September. People there talk about the "ber months", from September to December.
  8. Nine. They run from 16 to 24 December.
  9. 7 January, because 25 December in the Julian calendar currently falls on that date in the Gregorian one.
  10. 1576. A tree is also recorded in Strasbourg Cathedral in 1539, but the Turckheim picture is the earliest firmly dated image the article gives.

Which of these are customs and which are rules?

Most are habits that a family keeps or drops. Japan's KFC meal is a habit, not a law. Wigilia is closer to a rule in the homes that follow it. I mention this because trivia answers sound more official than they are. When you read an answer aloud, add "traditionally" and the table will forgive you.

Can you play Christmas trivia online with friends in another country?

It is a good fit, since the people most likely to know a tradition are the ones who live in it. Boardit's Trivia Tavern is something I build and offer free; it seats two to eight and everyone plays on their own screen. You get a trivia quiz for friends scattered across time zones, and each guest can add a custom of their own when the round ends.

My ten are not in the game. Its questions number over 2,500, grouped in nine categories, and Geography is the category closest to this subject. Rounds begin easy, get harder and close on a number question, as my question 10 does. The last round pays double, so a family member who guessed badly on Iceland still has a chance.

How do you build a round from these ten?

Split them into three groups. Questions 1, 5 and 6 are about things people can picture, so they make a gentle start. Questions 2, 3, 4 and 7 are the middle: customs that need a guess, with several plausible answers. Questions 8, 9 and 10 are for players who like numbers and dates.

If you run a longer trivia night, put this set after a general knowledge round and before a music round. A table that has just heard about thirteen pranksters is ready for carols. You can also give a bonus point for any player who can name a custom from their own country that is not on the list. That keeps the quiet guests involved, because they know something the quizmaster does not.

For a family with young children, drop question 10 and ask the youngest to guess the goat. They will enjoy the answer more than the year. The game works the same way: easy first, harder later, a number at the end of each round, and a winner who is not always the person with the most facts.

Where could you take the set next?

Ask each guest to bring one custom from their own home, with a source if they have one. You can turn the answers into next year's set. The strongest sets come from the people at the table, because they know which stories get told again.
