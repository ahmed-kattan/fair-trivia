Board games that teach economics: five that actually do

Ahmed Moaz · 16 October 2026

Five board games that model a real economic idea are the Landlord's Game, Power Grid, Catan, Acquire and Modern Art. Each puts one idea at the centre of play: rent, price, trade, ownership or value. I did not find a classroom study showing that any of them teaches economics, so read "actually do" as "build the idea into the rules". Whether students learn it depends on the questions you ask after the game.

The Landlord's Game: who gets the rent?

Elizabeth Magie patented the game in 1904. Wikipedia's article says it was meant to show how rents enrich property owners and impoverish tenants, an idea from the economist Henry George. It came with two rule sets. In the anti-monopolist version the game was won when the poorest player had doubled their original stake. In the monopolist version, you won by forming monopolies and forcing opponents out.

Play both rule sets with the same group and compare. What changes when the aim is shared prosperity? Who benefits from owning the scarce thing? Those are the questions of land rent, put on a board. The game is also the oldest on this list, so it makes a good opening: students can see the same economic argument turned into rules more than a century ago.

Power Grid: where prices come from

Friedemann Friese's game reached English in 2004. Players bid for power plants in an auction that opens at the plant's value and runs round the table until everyone passes. Then they buy fuel from a market in which each purchase makes the rest more expensive. Fuel is bought in reverse turn order, so the player furthest behind pays least.

That gives you supply and demand you can watch. When many players want the same fuel, the price climbs with every purchase; when plants run on something nobody else uses, the price stays low. Ask students to predict next round's fuel price, then check it. Ask why the game lets the trailing player buy first, and what would happen to the race if it did not. It is a heavy game, listed at two hours or more, so use it for a whole session rather than a lesson slot.

Catan: trade has to leave both sides better off

In Klaus Teuber's 1995 game, players trade resource cards with each other. The bank will swap four of one resource for one of another, and ports improve that rate to three or two for one.

My reading of that rule is that the bank rate is an outside option. No sensible player pays another player more than the bank would charge, so every offer happens between the bank's price and the price a partner would accept. That is the idea behind a bargaining range, and you can show it with four cards on a table.

Acquire: what ownership means

Sid Sackson's 1964 game has players found hotel chains, buy stock in them and merge them. The two largest shareholders of a chain that gets bought receive bonuses. Ask why the biggest owner of a small chain might want that chain to be taken over. The game shows the gap between running a company and owning it. It also shows why a merger can pay one owner and not another, which is a fair first lesson in who gains when a company is bought.

Modern Art: value that the market makes

Reiner Knizia's 1992 card game is built around auctions, and the card for sale decides which kind of auction is held. Each round, the value of an artist rises with the number of paintings sold, only the top three artists pay out, and values carry into later rounds. Nothing in the paintings makes them worth more. The sales do.

Ask: if you hold many paintings by an artist, should you sell them or hold them? Students find that the answer depends on what the others do.

Running the debrief

The game is the experiment and the talk afterwards is the lesson. A simple plan works with any of the five.

  1. Stop with ten minutes left and ask each player to write down one price they paid and one they would not pay again.
  2. Put the prices on a board and ask what they have in common.
  3. Ask which rule of the game caused the price, and what would change if that rule were removed.
  4. Finish with one real-world case: land rent, an electricity market, a merger, an art auction.

Ten minutes of talk like this is worth more than another round of play.

A sixth, from a table I build

Property Rush is a property trading game with real cities grouped by country, bought, rented and traded. It is an independent Monopoly alternative, and the economics sits in two rules. With the default rules, building a hotel hands the four houses it replaces back to the bank, which has a finite stock, so one player's upgrade can free building stock for another. And no round counter ends the game, so play continues until a single player remains. That is the monopolist ending of Magie's game: rent concentrating until the table is empty.
