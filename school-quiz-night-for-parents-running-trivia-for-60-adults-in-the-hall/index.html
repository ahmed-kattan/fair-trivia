School quiz night for parents: running trivia for 60 adults in the hall

Ahmed Moaz · 17 October 2026

Sixty parents, one hall, one microphone and a PTA volunteer who has never run a quiz: that is the usual school quiz night. For a first-timer learning how to run a quiz night, three things need fixing in advance: the size of the teams, the length of each round and what happens when two teams tie. Here is a plan for all three, with a short quizmaster script for the trivia quiz.

How many people per quiz team?

Five or six. Sixty adults make ten teams of six, or twelve teams of five.

The reason to keep teams small is older than pub quizzes. In 1979 Bibb Latané, Kipling Williams and Stephen Harkins studied what is now called social loafing. People shouted less hard when they believed others were shouting with them. As the study is usually summarised, effort fell to 82% of solo effort with one partner and to 74% with five.

A quiz is not a shouting test. Still, the pattern is familiar to anyone who has sat at the far end of a team of ten, watching two people fill in the answer sheet.

A bigger team also struggles to hear itself across a round table in a noisy hall. Ten teams keep marking manageable, too.

How many questions in a quiz night, and how long should a quiz round be?

Six rounds of eight questions, 48 in all, fit comfortably into an evening.

Here is how the timing works out. Say the category, read the question twice, then allow about 30 seconds to write. Call it a minute a question.

Eight questions take eight minutes. Add two minutes for teams to swap papers and mark, and each round is about ten minutes. That is my plan, not a timed evening.

TimeWhat happens
0:00Teams find tables, name themselves, pay in
0:15Rounds 1 to 3
0:45Break: food, drinks, raffle, scores read out
1:00Rounds 4 to 6
1:30Tie-break if needed, results, thanks
1:45Close

An hour and three quarters keeps the evening inside babysitter territory. If you need to cut, cut a round, not the break. Among school fundraiser ideas, a quiz is cheap to stage: paper, pens and a microphone. The break is when the raffle and the food sell, so protect it.

A quizmaster script for a school quiz night

The script matters more than the questions. Parents forgive a hard question; they do not forgive one they could not hear.

  1. "One answer sheet per team. Phones away until the break."
  2. "I'll say the category first, then read each question twice."
  3. "Swap sheets with the table on your left to mark."
  4. "My decision on spelling is final, and I'll be generous."

The second line borrows from Trivia Tavern, the quiz game I make. It names each question's category for 2.4 seconds before the question itself appears. That short pause gives every player a moment to switch subjects before the clock matters. A quizmaster can do the same out loud: "Next one is geography." Pause. Then read.

Where the questions come from

Writing 48 good trivia questions is the hardest job of the night. It usually lands on one volunteer a week before.

Published question lists, trivia online included, are a shortcut for one round or more. The quiz I build runs in a browser on Boardit. Trivia Tavern is built for playing with friends, and its topics are general knowledge rather than curriculum, which is right for a room of parents with nothing in common but the school. The quiz is for the adults in the hall: Boardit is not directed at children under 13.

For a PTA quiz night, keep one round local: the school's history, the head teacher's career, the year the hall was built. Those questions are the ones parents talk about at the gate the next morning.

The quiz night tie breaker

Ties happen, and the worst time to write a tie-break question is while two teams stand waiting. Trivia Tavern draws five spare questions at the start of every match, so sudden death always has questions waiting. Copy that. Keep five tie-break questions in an envelope before the doors open.

Make them numerical, closest answer wins: how many steps lead up to the school's front door, or the year the school opened. A number almost never ties twice.

What this plan has not been tested on

The timings above are arithmetic, not a stopwatch on a real trivia night. The case for small teams rests on a 1979 shouting experiment, not on quizzes. And nobody has measured how 60 parents pace in a hall. Run the first round, check the clock, and adjust the rest of the evening from there.
