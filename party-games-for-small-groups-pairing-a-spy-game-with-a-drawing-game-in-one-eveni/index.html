Party games for small groups: pairing a spy game with a drawing game in one evening

Ahmed Moaz · 4 November 2026

Most party games for small groups are good at one thing. A spy game, the sort people call an imposter game, is good at talk and suspicion. A drawing game is good at noise and bad art. Put them back to back and each covers the other's weak spot. I make both of them on Boardit, so I can add up the clocks and show what the evening costs in minutes.

Why do these two fit together?

They use opposite muscles. In the spy game you speak in careful short phrases and watch faces. In the drawing game you say nothing at all, and the loudest sound is a guess typed into chat.

That swap matters after twenty minutes. A table tired from lying and hunting for liars is glad to switch to scribbling. A table tired from scribbling is glad to sit back and talk. Starting with either one works, but I like the spy game first, because the quiet suspicion warms up a group that has just arrived.

How long will the pair take?

Here are the ceilings, from the engines' own clocks. A drawing is up to 100 seconds by default, and a match is three rounds in which everyone draws once per round.

TableSpy game, 3 roundsDrawing game, 3 rounds
4 playersup to 15.5 minup to 20 min (12 drawings)
6 playersup to 20.5 minup to 30 min (18 drawings)

So four friends need about 36 minutes of actual play, six need around 51. The real figure is usually lower, since the drawing figure assumes every clock runs out, and the spy figure assumes the table stops at its first chance to vote. Leave a gap between the two games for snacks and for setting up the second room.

What should the first game decide?

A small, silly rule makes the pair feel like one night. For example: whoever was caught as the spy opens the drawing game, or whoever scored least in the first game picks the pack for the second. It costs nothing, and it gives the two games a thread.

The drawing game also lets the host add words of their own, kept hidden from the players. A shared in-joke from the spy round can turn up there as a word, which makes the second game feel like a sequel.

Where do you start?

Boardit's Who's the Spy is the natural opener, since it needs only three players and plays as a spy game online or on one shared phone.

When the spy match ends, open the drawing game and start a fresh room for the same group. The drawing table goes from two to twelve seats, and a friend can still join once it is under way.

How do the scores compare across the two?

They don't carry over, and that is a good thing. In the spy game you earn single points for a correct vote, for escaping the vote, or for finding the word. In the drawing game a quick guess is worth up to 500 points and a last-second one 100. Keep each game's board separate, or agree on a prize for the one who won more of them.

If you want a combined score, give rank points: three for first, two for second, one for third in each game. It takes a minute to add up and keeps the loser of one game in the running.

What goes wrong with this pairing?

  • The second game starts late. Open the next room while the last scores are on screen.
  • One person dominates both. The spy game rotates turn order and the spy draw avoids repeats, but a strong drawer is still strong. Let them draw last.
  • The group is too small. The spy game needs three, so with two, play only the drawing game.
