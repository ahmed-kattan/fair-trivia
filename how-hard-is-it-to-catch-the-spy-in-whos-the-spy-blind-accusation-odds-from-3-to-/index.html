How hard is it to catch the spy in Who's the Spy? Blind accusation odds from 3 to 8 players

Ahmed Moaz · 20 October 2026

How hard is it to catch the spy in Who's the Spy? If nobody listened and everyone voted at random, how often would the table hit the right person? The answer depends only on the size of the table, and the numbers set a baseline against which good play can be measured. Here is the table, with what it means.

What is the rule?

You cannot vote for yourself. So a civilian who has no idea chooses among the other players, which is every player but themselves. With one spy, the chance that a blind vote names the spy is one in the number of other players: one divided by n minus one, where n is the table size.

What are the odds?

  • 3 players: 1 in 2, 50%
  • 4 players: 1 in 3, 33.3%
  • 5 players: 1 in 4, 25%
  • 6 players: 1 in 5, 20%
  • 7 players: 1 in 6, 16.7%
  • 8 players: 1 in 7, 14.3%

These are the chances for each voter acting alone. The expected points from the "name a spy" route per civilian per round, if everyone voted blindly, are the same numbers: 0.5 of a point at three players, 0.143 at eight.

What does it tell us?

That a small table is easy to read by luck, and a large one is hard. At three, the spy is caught half the time even without any thought. At eight, a blind vote misses the spy six times in seven. So at a large table, the clues carry the weight; at a small one, the odds already help the civilians.

And the chance that a random player is the spy?

One divided by the number of players: 33.3% at three, 25% at four, 20% at five, 16.7% at six, 14.3% at seven and 12.5% at eight. With two spies at eight, it doubles to 25%.

What does good play add?

A lot. Clues, hesitations and tells narrow the field. A table that listens turns a one-in-seven guess into a good chance of catching the spy. The baseline is the floor; skill is everything above it.

How does the vote rule interact?

Nobody can vote for themselves, which removes one useless option. A tie goes to a runoff between the tied players, and a second tie ejects nobody. For the spy, that last rule is a lifeline: if the table splits evenly in the runoff, the spy escapes and scores.

How do the points reflect the odds?

A voter who names a spy scores a point, and a spy who is not ejected scores one too. At a big table, where blind votes mostly miss, the civilians have to work for their points, and a spy who escapes is common. The scoring pays the work of listening.

Is the spy at a disadvantage?

At three or four, yes. At six and above, the spy has a good chance of surviving a vote, and a better one if they say little. That is why the host can add a second spy at big tables: to give the civilians something to hunt.

What would a perfect listener score?

I have no measured figure for real play, so I will not invent one. The direction is clear, though: attention lifts the hit rate well above the blind baseline. If you track your own table's hit rate over twenty rounds, you will see where you stand, and you may be surprised how often a calm spy escapes.

Where can you test it?

At Board It, a free imposter game and spy game online with friends, you can compare the odds with real results by keeping a tally over several evenings. Three to eight can play, or twelve around one phone.

A closing comparison

If you win more often than the table says you should, you are listening. If you win less, you are talking too much.
